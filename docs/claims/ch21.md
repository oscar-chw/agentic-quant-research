# Chapter 21. Brute force and quantum computers, item by item

v1 (docs/afml_sections/ch21.md) marked the whole chapter not applicable, because a Polymarket bet is one position in
one 5-minute binary token and there is no multi-asset trajectory to optimise. v2 separates two different questions. The
chapter's *method* — discretise a non-convex problem, enumerate every feasible answer, keep the best — is implementable
and is implemented in `src/pmlab/afml/trajectory.py`, with `tests/test_afml_trajectory.py` checking the pigeonhole
count, the full-investment constraint, the cost and Sharpe formulas, and the exhaustive search against an independent
enumeration written a second time in the test. The chapter's *hardware* claim — that a quantum computer would evaluate
all of the trajectories at once — has no counterpart: docs/hardware.md records no accelerators, so every trajectory is
scored one at a time.

The size that decides everything is measured, not asserted -- and the audit's second reading re-measured it, because
the first pass had multiplied one core's rate by four. Scoring runs at about 119,000 trajectories a second on one core
and about 313,000 on the 4 workers docs/hardware.md allows: a 2.6-fold speed-up, not the 4-fold one the arithmetic
assumed (docs/hardware.md, "Measured"). The section's own numerical example with K equal to 10 units, N equal to 5
assets and H equal to 4 horizons has 32,032 feasible weight vectors and therefore 1.05e18 trajectories: 9.3e8 hours of
the 4 workers, about 107,000 years. Dropping the duplicates the construction produces (I21.33) leaves 14,002 distinct
vectors and 3.84e16 trajectories, still about 3,900 years. The largest case that fits inside the "hours, not days"
ceiling of docs/hardware.md is K equal to 8, N equal to 4, H equal to 3: 1,408 distinct vectors, 2.79e9 trajectories,
about 2.5 hours. The tests run a case far smaller than that so the suite stays under a second.
